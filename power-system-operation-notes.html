1. Economic dispatch with output limits

Question

Three generators supply a total demand of 850 MW. Neglect transmission losses. Determine the minimum-cost dispatch, the system incremental cost, and the total hourly production cost.

Unit Output limits (MW) Cost Ci(Pi) ($/h)
G1 150 ≤ P₁ ≤ 600 459 + 6.48P₁ + 0.00128P₁²
G2 100 ≤ P₂ ≤ 400 310 + 7.85P₂ + 0.00194P₂²
G3 50 ≤ P₃ ≤ 200 78 + 7.97P₃ + 0.00482P₃²

Solution

1. Write the objective and the balance equation

Minimise C₁(P₁) + C₂(P₂) + C₃(P₃)
P₁ + P₂ + P₃ = 850 MW

For a generator operating strictly inside its limits, its incremental cost equals λ. Differentiating removes the constant terms, but those terms must be included when calculating total cost.

C′₁ = 6.48 + 0.00256P₁ = λ
C′₂ = 7.85 + 0.00388P₂ = λ
C′₃ = 7.97 + 0.00964P₃ = λ

2. Solve first without the output limits

(λ − 6.48)/0.00256 + (λ − 7.85)/0.00388
+ (λ − 7.97)/0.00964 = 850
λ = 8.285176 $/MWh
Unit Calculated output (MW) Check
G1 705.1467 Above 600 MW
G2 112.1587 Within limits
G3 32.6946 Below 50 MW

3. Test the apparent boundary solution

Temporarily set P₁ = 600 MW and P₃ = 50 MW. Power balance gives P₂ = 200 MW. This is feasible, but it is not yet optimal.

C′₁(600) = 6.48 + 0.00256 × 600 = 8.016
C′₂(200) = 7.85 + 0.00388 × 200 = 8.626
C′₃(50) = 7.97 + 0.00964 × 50 = 8.452

G3 can supply the next MW more cheaply than G2. Increasing P₃ while reducing P₂ therefore reduces cost. Release G3 from its lower limit; keep G1 at its upper limit.

4. Redispatch G2 and G3

P₂ + P₃ = 850 − 600 = 250
7.85 + 0.00388P₂ = 7.97 + 0.00964P₃
0.00388P₂ − 0.00964(250 − P₂) = 0.12
0.01352P₂ = 2.53
P₂ = 187.130178 MW
P₃ = 250 − 187.130178 = 62.869822 MW
λ = 7.85 + 0.00388 × 187.130178
= 8.576065 $/MWh

5. Check optimality and calculate cost

G2 and G3 are within their limits and have equal incremental costs. G1 has C′₁ = 8.016 < λ and is already at its maximum: the lower-cost unit cannot increase output further.

Ctotal = [459 + 6.48(600) + 0.00128(600)²]
+ [310 + 7.85(187.130178) + 0.00194(187.130178)²]
+ [78 + 7.97(62.869822) + 0.00482(62.869822)²]
= $7,252.83/h
Final answer

P₁ = 600 MW, P₂ = 187.130 MW, P₃ = 62.870 MW.
λ = 8.5761 $/MWh; total cost = $7,252.83/h.

2. Unit commitment and shutdown decisions

Question

Use the following priority list to schedule four generating units over six one-hour periods. Priority 1 is the first unit to be committed. Where a unit can be taken out of service, compare banking (keeping it hot) with cooling and restarting.

Unit / priority Rating (MW) Minimum down time (h) Banking ($/h) a (h⁻¹)
G1 / 1 600 2.00 1200 0.30
G2 / 2 300 1.25 1400 0.40
G3 / 3 300 1.50 800 0.15
G4 / 4 200 1.50 800 0.15
Restart cost = 6,400(1 − e−at) dollars
Hour 1 2 3 4 5 6
Demand (MW) 1300 1100 850 580 920 1250

Here t is the cooling duration in hours. Follow the lecture example: use the stated capacities, priority order, down times and costs; no additional reserve requirement is specified.

Solution

1. Build the cumulative-capacity list

Online units Maximum output (MW)
G1 600
G1 + G2 600 + 300 = 900
G1 + G2 + G3 900 + 300 = 1,200
G1 + G2 + G3 + G4 1,200 + 200 = 1,400

2. Form a provisional schedule from demand

Hour Demand (MW) Capacity-only choice Reason
1 1300 G1 + G2 + G3 + G4 1,200 < 1,300 ≤ 1,400
2 1100 G1 + G2 + G3 900 < 1,100 ≤ 1,200
3 850 G1 + G2 600 < 850 ≤ 900
4 580 G1 580 ≤ 600
5 920 G1 + G2 + G3 900 < 920 ≤ 1,200
6 1250 G1 + G2 + G3 + G4 1,200 < 1,250 ≤ 1,400

3. Check how long each unit would be off

  • G4: not needed in hours 2–5. Available down time = 4 h ≥ 1.5 h; it may be taken out of service.
  • G3: not needed in hours 3–4. Available down time = 2 h ≥ 1.5 h; it may be taken out of service.
  • G2: not needed only in hour 4. Available down time = 1 h < 1.25 h; keep G2 online in hour 4.

4. Compare banking and cooling costs

G4: four-hour interval.

Banking = 800 × 4 = $3,200.00
Cooling/restart = 6,400(1 − e−0.15×4)
= 6,400(1 − 0.5488116) = $2,887.61
Saving by cooling = 3,200 − 2,887.61 = $312.39

Cool G4 and restart it for hour 6.

G3: two-hour interval.

Banking = 800 × 2 = $1,600.00
Cooling/restart = 6,400(1 − e−0.15×2)
= 6,400(1 − 0.7408182) = $1,658.76
Saving by banking = 1,658.76 − 1,600 = $58.76

Keep G3 banked for hours 3–4, then return it to generation for hour 5.

5. Write the final schedule

Hour Online and generating Other unit status
1 G1 + G2 + G3 + G4 —
2 G1 + G2 + G3 G4 cooling / off
3 G1 + G2 G3 banked; G4 off
4 G1 + G2 G3 banked; G4 off
5 G1 + G2 + G3 G4 off
6 G1 + G2 + G3 + G4 G4 restarted
Final answer

Keep G2 online throughout. Bank G3 for 2 h. Cool G4 for 4 h and restart it for hour 6.
Cost of the two chosen layover strategies: $1,600 + $2,887.61 = $4,487.61 (not the total generation cost).

3. Dynamic programming for unit commitment

Question

Each online unit must operate between half and full rated output. Initially G1 and G2 are online. There are no minimum up/down times. Use four one-hour periods, neglect shutdown costs and all other unlisted factors.

Unit Rating (MW) Minimum (MW) Running cost ($/MWh) Startup ($)
G1 300 150 20 2000
G2 200 100 25 1800
G3 200 100 30 1600
Hour 1 2 3 4
Demand (MW) 500 600 250 450

Solution

1. Define the states and reject infeasible ones

State Online units Total minimum (MW) Total maximum (MW)
1 G3 100 200
2 G2 100 200
3 G1 150 300
4 G2 + G3 200 400
5 G1 + G3 250 500
6 G1 + G2 250 500
7 G1 + G2 + G3 350 700

A state is feasible only if its total minimum ≤ demand ≤ total maximum. Thus the feasible states are {5,6,7}, {7}, {3,4,5,6}, and {5,6,7} for hours 1–4, respectively.

2. Dispatch each feasible state

Start all online units at their minimum outputs, then allocate remaining demand in increasing running-cost order: G1, G2, G3. For example, at 600 MW with all units online, the dispatch is (300,200,100) MW and the hourly cost is 20×300 + 25×200 + 30×100 = $14,000.

Hour State (P₁, P₂, P₃) MW Running cost for 1 h ($)
1 5 (300, 0, 200) 12,000
1 6 (300, 200, 0) 11,000
1 7 (300, 100, 100) 11,500
2 7 (300, 200, 100) 14,000
3 3 (250, 0, 0) 5,000
3 4 (0, 150, 100) 6,750
3 5 (150, 0, 100) 6,000
3 6 (150, 100, 0) 5,500
4 5 (300, 0, 150) 10,500
4 6 (300, 150, 0) 9,750
4 7 (250, 100, 100) 10,500

3. Add only the required startup costs

Fh(s) = Ch(s) × 1 h
+ minr [Fh−1(r) + S(r,s)]
S(r,s) = sum of startup costs for units off in r but on in s

Initial state: F₀(6) = 0. G1 and G2 are already online, so do not charge their startup costs at hour 1.

F₁(5) = 12,000 + 1,600 = 13,600
F₁(6) = 11,000 + 0 = 11,000
F₁(7) = 11,500 + 1,600 = 13,100

4. Calculate hours 2 and 3

F₂(7) = 14,000 + min(13,600 + 1,800,
11,000 + 1,600, 13,100 + 0)
= 14,000 + 12,600 = 26,600; predecessor 6

At hour 3, the previous state is 7, so every feasible state can be reached by shutting units down without a startup cost.

F₃(3) = 26,600 + 5,000 = 31,600
F₃(4) = 26,600 + 6,750 = 33,350
F₃(5) = 26,600 + 6,000 = 32,600
F₃(6) = 26,600 + 5,500 = 32,100

5. Compare every hour-4 transition

Each entry is F₃(previous state) + startup cost + hour-4 running cost. This table includes all feasible predecessors, not just the path eventually selected.

Previous state To state 5 To state 6 To state 7
3 31,600 + 1,600 + 10,500 = 43,700 31,600 + 1,800 + 9,750 = 43,150 31,600 + 3,400 + 10,500 = 45,500
4 33,350 + 2,000 + 10,500 = 45,850 33,350 + 2,000 + 9,750 = 45,100 33,350 + 2,000 + 10,500 = 45,850
5 32,600 + 0 + 10,500 = 43,100 32,600 + 1,800 + 9,750 = 44,150 32,600 + 1,800 + 10,500 = 44,900
6 32,100 + 1,600 + 10,500 = 44,200 32,100 + 0 + 9,750 = 41,850 32,100 + 1,600 + 10,500 = 44,200
Minimum 43,100 (from 5) 41,850 (from 6) 44,200 (from 6)

6. Backtrack and verify

Initial state 6 → hour 1: 6 → hour 2: 7
→ hour 3: 6 → hour 4: 6
Total = 11,000 + (14,000 + 1,600)
+ 5,500 + 9,750 = $41,850
Final answer

Online units by hour: {G1,G2} → {G1,G2,G3} → {G1,G2} → {G1,G2}.
Only G3 is started, at hour 2. Minimum four-hour cost = $41,850.

4. Under-frequency load shedding

Question

A 50 Hz system initially supplies 13,000 MW with 13,000 MW of generation capacity. At t = 0, it loses 3,000 MW of generation. Use H = 5 s and load-damping constant D = 1.0. Ignore relay delays and governor response in this example.

UFLS stage Threshold (Hz) Load shed (% of original load)
1 49.0 3.077%
2 48.8 10%
3 48.6 10%

Find the frequency response without UFLS, the operating time of each UFLS stage, and the response after shedding.

Solution

1. Select the base and convert to per unit

Sbase = 13,000 − 3,000 = 10,000 MVA
ΔPm = −3,000 / 10,000 = −0.3 pu
PL0 = 13,000 / 10,000 = 1.3 pu
DT = PL0D = 1.3
Δω = (f − 50)/50

Generation loss is negative. Define q = −ΔPL > 0 as the positive amount of load shed. Following the slides, 3.077% is rounded to 400 MW.

Stage Load shed (MW) q (pu) Damping after stage
1 0.03077 × 13,000 ≈ 400 0.04 (1.3 − 0.04) × 1 = 1.26
2 0.10 × 13,000 = 1,300 0.13 (1.3 − 0.04 − 0.13) × 1 = 1.13
3 0.10 × 13,000 = 1,300 0.13 (1.3 − 0.04 − 0.13 − 0.13) × 1 = 1.00

2. Solve the response without UFLS

2H dΔω/dt + DTΔω = ΔPm
10 dΔω/dt + 1.3Δω = −0.3
Δω(t) = (−0.3/1.3)(1 − e−0.13t)
f(t) = 50[1 − (0.3/1.3)(1 − e−0.13t)] Hz

The initial rate of change and the mathematical steady value are:

df/dt at t = 0 = 50(−0.3)/(2×5) = −1.5 Hz/s
f(∞) = 50(1 − 0.3/1.3) = 38.4615 Hz

3. Find the first threshold crossing

At 49 Hz: Δω₁ = (49 − 50)/50 = −0.020
−0.020 = (−0.3/1.3)(1 − e−0.13t₁)
e−0.13t₁ = 1 − (1.3/−0.3)(−0.020)
= 0.913333333
t₁ = −ln(0.913333333)/0.13
= 0.697341 s ≈ 0.7 s

4. Follow the slide superposition method for stage 2

For this section, use the lecture’s adjusted-damping superposition: after each shedding event, evaluate all active event responses using the new damping value. Retain unrounded event times.

Δω(t) = −(0.3/1.26)(1 − e−0.126t)
+ (0.04/1.26)(1 − e−0.126(t−t₁))

To solve without trial-and-error, collect the exponential terms. Write Δω(t) = A + B e−kt.

A = (−0.3 + 0.04)/1.26 = −0.206349206
B = [0.3 − 0.04e0.126×0.697341]/1.26
= 0.203433628
k = 1.26/10 = 0.126
Δω₂ = (48.8 − 50)/50 = −0.024
t₂ = −ln[(−0.024 − A)/B]/0.126
= 0.868383 s ≈ 0.9 s

5. Repeat for stage 3

Δω(t) = −(0.3/1.13)(1 − e−0.113t)
+ (0.04/1.13)(1 − e−0.113(t−t₁))
+ (0.13/1.13)(1 − e−0.113(t−t₂))
A = (−0.3 + 0.04 + 0.13)/1.13 = −0.115044248
B = [0.3 − 0.04e0.113×0.697341
− 0.13e0.113×0.868383]/1.13
= 0.100280613
Δω₃ = (48.6 − 50)/50 = −0.028
t₃ = −ln[(−0.028 − A)/B]/0.113
= 1.252706 s ≈ 1.25 s

6. Write the response after the third stage

The damping becomes 1.0. The lecture-method expression for t > t₃ is:

Δω(t) = −0.3(1 − e−0.1t)
+ 0.04(1 − e−0.1(t−t₁))
+ 0.13(1 − e−0.1(t−t₂))
+ 0.13(1 − e−0.1(t−t₃))
f(t) = 50[1 + Δω(t)]
Δω(∞) = −0.3 + 0.04 + 0.13 + 0.13 = 0
f(∞) = 50 Hz
Final answer

Using the slide method with unrounded intermediate times:
t₁ = 0.69734 s, t₂ = 0.86838 s, t₃ = 1.25271 s.
Total load shed ≈ 3,000 MW; the model tends back to 50 Hz.

5. Least-squares state estimation

Question

Estimate the state x = [v₁, v₂, e]ᵀ of the DC circuit. Node 3 is the reference node (v₃ = 0). All three resistors are 1 Ω.

  • Branch 1→2 contains R₁ and a series source e, whose positive terminal faces node 1: i₁₂ = (v₁ − v₂ − e)/R₁.
  • R₂ connects nodes 3 and 1; measured current i₃₁ is positive from node 3 to node 1.
  • R₃ connects nodes 2 and 3; measured current i₂₃ is positive from node 2 to node 3.
Measurement Value
i₁₂ 1.0 A
i₃₁ −3.2 A
i₂₃ 0.8 A
e 1.1 V

Treat all four measurements with equal numerical weights, as in the lecture example. Form the least-squares problem, estimate the three states, and calculate the residual vector.

Solution

1. Write one equation per measurement

v₁ − v₂ − e ≈ 1.0
−v₁ ≈ −3.2
v₂ ≈ 0.8
e ≈ 1.1

If the last three measurements were exact, the first predicted current would be 3.2 − 0.8 − 1.1 = 1.3 A, not 1.0 A. The four measured equations are inconsistent, so minimise their squared residuals.

2. Assemble A, x and b

A = 1−1−1−100010001

x = [v₁, v₂, e]ᵀ
b = [1.0, −3.2, 0.8, 1.1]ᵀ
Ax ≈ b

3. Form the least-squares objective

r = b − Ax
J(x) = ½(b − Ax)ᵀ(b − Ax)
∂J/∂x = −Aᵀ(b − Ax) = 0
AᵀA x̂ = Aᵀb

4. Calculate the normal-equation coefficients

AᵀA = 2−1−1−121−112

Aᵀb = [1(1.0) + (−1)(−3.2),
　　　 (−1)(1.0) + 1(0.8),
　　　 (−1)(1.0) + 1(1.1)]ᵀ
　　 = [4.2, −0.2, 0.1]ᵀ

5. Solve the three simultaneous equations

2v₁ − v₂ − e = 4.2　　　　　 (1)
−v₁ + 2v₂ + e = −0.2　　　　(2)
−v₁ + v₂ + 2e = 0.1　　　　 (3)

Add equations (1)+(2), add (1)+(3), then subtract (2) from (3):

v₁ + v₂ = 4.0 → v₁ = 4.0 − v₂
v₁ + e = 4.3
e − v₂ = 0.3 → e = v₂ + 0.3

Substitute v₁ = 4 − v₂ and e = v₂ + 0.3 into equation (2):

−(4 − v₂) + 2v₂ + (v₂ + 0.3) = −0.2
4v₂ − 3.7 = −0.2
v₂ = 3.5/4 = 0.875 V
v₁ = 4 − 0.875 = 3.125 V
e = 0.875 + 0.3 = 1.175 V

6. Reconstruct the measurements and calculate residuals

A x̂ = [3.125 − 0.875 − 1.175,
　　　 −3.125, 0.875, 1.175]ᵀ
　　 = [1.075, −3.125, 0.875, 1.175]ᵀ

r = b − A x̂
　= [1.0 − 1.075, −3.2 − (−3.125),
　　 0.8 − 0.875, 1.1 − 1.175]ᵀ
　= [−0.075, −0.075, −0.075, −0.075]ᵀ

The first three residuals have units of amperes and the fourth has units of volts. The example assigns equal numerical weights to these measurement equations.

Jmin = ½ × 4 × (0.075)² = 0.01125
Aᵀr = [0, 0, 0]ᵀ
Final answer

v₁ = 3.125 V, v₂ = 0.875 V, e = 1.175 V.
The residuals are −0.075 A, −0.075 A, −0.075 A and −0.075 V, in the stated measurement order.
